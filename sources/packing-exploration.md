# Packing exercises prompted by the unit-disk question

The seed problem was supplied by Beniamin Bogosel in conversation on 15 October 2026: maximize the number of unit-width bodies with disjoint interiors in the unit disk, with three Reuleaux triangles suggested through a 120-degree argument. Here **unit disk means radius one**, and boundary contact is allowed.

The answer and equality case are established in [Three unit-width bodies in the unit disk](https://beniamin-bogosel.github.io/constant-width-problems/#cw-unit-disk-packing). The logical order matters: every body first contains the disk center, and each then needs **at least** 120 degrees there. Only a packing of three forces every angle to equal 120 degrees. An arbitrary single admissible body need not fit in a 120-degree sector; a centered disk is an immediate example.

## Main conclusions

- At most three bodies fit, even when their shapes differ. All maximizing triples consist of Reuleaux triangles with a vertex at the disk center and tangent sectors partitioning the full angle. The arrangement is unique up to a common rotation or reflection and relabeling.
- Every prescribed unit-width shape admits two translated copies in the disk. It admits three congruent copies precisely when it is a Reuleaux triangle. This classifies the packing number for each fixed shape, including smooth shapes.
- The same three-triangle arrangement maximizes total occupied area, with area `3(π−√3)/2`, approximately 67.30% of the disk.
- If closed bodies must be disjoint, rather than merely having disjoint interiors, only one fits: every admissible body contains the center.
- Four unit-width bodies require radius at least `(1+√2)(1−1/√3) ≈ 1.020367`. Four half-radius disks give the upper bound `(1+√2)/2 ≈ 1.207107`. No exact value of the four-body optimum is asserted.
- For lattice translates of a prescribed width-w body K, the optimal density is `2 area(K)/(√3 w²)`. This restricted packing problem favors the disk. It must not be confused with arbitrary packings allowing independent rotations.

## Related short ideas

The first three new exercises belong to Generalities: an enclosing ball forces a concentric inner ball, farthest distance equals width plus signed distance to the body, and the locus of covering-ball centers is an inner or outer parallel body. These support the packing exercises in Chapter 6.

The translate collision criterion follows from the difference body and is valid in every Euclidean dimension. The common-point angle argument is planar and permits unequal widths. The final counterexample shows why diameter one cannot replace constant width one: arbitrarily many thin full-dimensional triangles fit in the disk.

All ten exercises have explicit hints and complete proof drafts. They are derived here from the user's proposal and established lemmas already in the collection, without a claim of mathematical novelty. Substantial inputs, such as the sharp planar inradius bound, are identified in statements and prerequisite links.
