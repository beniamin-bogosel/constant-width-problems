# Exercises from *Mixed volumes and the Blaschke–Lebesgue theorem*

Source: Beniamin Bogosel, [arXiv:2311.14618](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14618), published in *Acta Mathematica Hungarica* 173, 122–138 (2024), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10474-024-01435-w).

The inspected local PDF is the 16-page preprint dated **2 May 2024**. All page, equation, lemma, and theorem numbers below refer to that version, not the journal pagination. Its path and SHA-256 fingerprint are recorded in `local-inventory.json`; the paper itself is not redistributed. Statements and proofs were rewritten as standalone exercises, with alternative proofs where useful. The new entries remain checked drafts awaiting human review.

This extraction adds **19 exercises** to Chapter 4, bringing that chapter to **25 entries**. Four previously present entries now also cite the paper: the reflected mixed-area complement identity, Blaschke’s spatial volume formula, the symmetric-cover bound, and the mixed-area proof of Blaschke–Lebesgue (the last remains in Chapter 3).

## Scope and mathematical qualifications

- The polygon edge formula is a preparatory exercise. The segment, cap, and polygon arguments lead to the full inclusion-interval statement of Theorem 2, including irregular Reuleaux polygons. The proof of that theorem uses polygon support measures rather than the paper’s curved-parallelogram decomposition.
- Skeleton extrema use the planar Rogers–Shephard inequality as an explicitly allowed theorem. Its proof is not hidden inside a short solution. Arc-angle bounds and the regular-polygon construction are also stated or linked.
- The planar hull identity is proved using disjoint positive parts of support-function differences. The needed spatial identity is then proved by averaging planar projections and using Cauchy’s projection formula; this avoids assuming an arbitrary three-dimensional analogue of the area identity.
- The 2D and 3D reverse-isoperimetric objectives are proved equivalent to constant-width optimization over hulls arising from constant-width bodies. Enlarging the feasible class to all bodies in the indicated annulus is a relaxation. Exactness in dimension three is not claimed.
- The planar annulus optimization theorem cited from Bianchini–Henrot in the paper is not re-proved here. The new exercises establish the annulus inclusions and why neither inclusion can simply be dropped.
- The 3D inradius lower bound uses Jung’s theorem as an allowed input. The historical Chakerian estimate is presented as a valid estimate, without asserting it is currently the strongest known bound.
- The paper’s final research questions were not automatically added as currently open problems. They need separate status checks and careful distinctions between a relaxation and equality of its optimal value with the original problem.

## Normalizations and corrections in the inspected version

1. In the half-disk example on p. 6, the diameter segment of the unit disk has length 2 and its perpendicular width is 2. Thus **A(segment, disk) = 2**, not 1. Also, Steiner’s formula gives A(K, B) = perimeter(K)/2; the displayed perimeter(B) in the preceding sentence is a typo. The exercise calculates the correction explicitly.
2. A few occurrences of `A` in the three-dimensional objective chain on p. 11 mean the trilinear mixed volume `V`. The exercises consistently use `V`, and distinguish surface area from perimeter.
3. On p. 12 the sentence about a body containing a regular tetrahedron concerns maximal **circumradius**, hence minimal inradius. The exercises state the applicable Jung bound and do not repeat the inradius wording.
4. The scaling argument after (16) gives a strictly negative derivative when the centered inradius is strictly larger than the inner radius. At contact, monotonicity alone gives a nonpositive derivative. The inner-contact exercise uses precisely the strict-gap argument.
5. The competitor argument in Theorem 9 proves a closed upper bound for the inradius. A strict exclusion at the threshold would require an equality argument. The threshold exercise retains the closed endpoint and explains why.
6. Skeletons of width-one Reuleaux triangles are equilateral triangles of **side** one; their own width is not one. The exercise wording uses the width of the Reuleaux polygon throughout.
